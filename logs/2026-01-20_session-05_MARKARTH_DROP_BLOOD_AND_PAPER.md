# Session 05 - MARKARTH DROP: BLOOD AND PAPER
Date: 2026-01-20
Act: 2
Scene: S5_HOUSE_OF_HORRORS → S8_MARKARTH_DROP_INTERDICTION
Hold: The Reach
Location: Markarth — Abandoned House / Undercity Sluice Tunnels / Understone Keep

The Reach does not do "quiet victories." It does stone, blood, and consequences.

Agran Moorcroft arrived at Markarth with Imperial purpose in his posture and predation folded neatly behind a bard's smile. Hadvar—shield-brother, courier, friend—had been stabilized at the checkpoint after the Switchback ambush, but poison and sabotage do not end just because a man survives. The road to Solitude was still the objective, and yet Markarth insisted on making itself the hinge of the story.

At the city gate, a Vigilant of Stendarr named Tyranus stood before a boarded house and spoke of a voice inside. Agran heard enough in the air to believe him. Some threats announce themselves with steel. This one announced itself with authority.

The Abandoned House was not abandoned. It was a contract in wood and stone—a Daedric courtroom disguised as rot. In the cellar, iron bars held Logrolf, a priest of Boethiah, and an altar held a weapon suspended like a verdict waiting to be read. Molag Bal's presence was not subtle; it was pressure. It was a demand that someone in the room prove domination is real.

Tyranus tried first: faith, hammer, and refusal. But the cage was not merely metal. It was mechanism and rite, designed to make every "clean" solution fail. Agran named the trap for what it was and rallied Tyranus into moving as one—shield-brothers in function, even if not in belief. For a moment, zeal and pragmatism held together without snapping.

Then Agran took the mace.

The Mace of Molag Bal does not feel like weight. It feels like inevitability—like holding a law that wants to be enforced. The house demanded Agran kneel. It did not get the kneel, but resistance earned notice all the same. Tyranus recoiled from the artifact; Logrolf smiled through fear with the wrong kind of awareness. The cellar waited for cruelty like it was the only language it respected.

Agran's choice was not to torture. It was to end the lever.

Logrolf died to the mace—swift, brutal, definitive. The house accepted the conclusion with the cold satisfaction of a trap that got at least one bite. The latch upstairs clicked open. Molag Bal's laughter did not echo; it settled. A champion's path had been offered, and whether Agran intended it or not, the Prince had learned the shape of his ambition.

Tyranus did not strike Agran down. He should have, by doctrine. But he had lived through the cellar too, and survival creates its own treaties. Agran argued custody as an Imperial necessity—Solitude vaults, Legion containment—and Tyranus agreed, stiffly, warning that if the mace whispered, he would hear of it. They parted with an uneasy truce: not friendship, not forgiveness, but the kind of handshake that keeps a man from becoming a hunt.

Legate Fasendil, who had delegated the "house problem" while being "busy," met Agran with the Imperial officer's version of gratitude—real, begrudging, and edged with irritation that the world required it. Agran's missive remained intended for General Tullius in Solitude, but Markarth could offer what the road needed: safe passage, men, and intelligence.

The phrase "MARKARTH DROP" proved real. Not rumor—procedure. A handoff point beneath the city in sluice tunnels stained by smelter runoff: "where the silver bleeds." Fasendil spoke of an Altmer "advisor" who appeared whenever patrols neared the truth, and of witnesses who forgot too conveniently afterward. That was the shape of the war beneath the war: Thalmor shadow work wearing clean gloves.

Hadvar's recovery came first in duty, but Agran prepared a second move. He ordered the checkpoint secured and a small team—marksman, warrior, nightblade—to meet him under Markarth by nightfall. On the road's edge, reality itself shivered: a Dwemer seam-door that had not existed yesterday hummed in recognition of the mace. Agran touched it. Time opened sideways.

In a stolen slice of timeline, Agran saw the Drop. A courier with a crescent-over-tower mark. A Markarth guard captain providing escort cover. An Altmer handler in immaculate gloves giving soft orders and naming the route: Markarth to a Dragon Bridge relay, then Solitude. Agran's own name surfaced as a problem to be removed. The seam snapped shut. The intel remained.

Nightfall in the tunnels became geometry and discipline. Agran's plan turned corridors into a kill-zone—exits choked, lights controlled, crossfire set. When the Drop arrived, Agran struck first, hard, and impossibly fast. The handler fell to the Mace of Molag Bal in one decisive arc, and the artifact drank enough of the moment to knit old pain under armor. The escorts collapsed under coordinated violence. The courier was restrained. The captain reached for a horn that never sounded.

Agran disarmed the horn with a crack of bone and then applied the sharper weapon: fear plus evidence. The satchel held a bribe ledger, a route slip, and a sealed packet forged to pass casual eyes. Under pressure—and under the private, horrifying certainty of what Agran briefly allowed the captain to see—the captain confessed. Names. Payments. The handler's alias. The relay contact at Dragon Bridge. The compromised courier chain to Solitude. A confession letter was dictated, signed, and sealed.

More important than blood was the paper blade: Agran established a formal chain-of-custody—witnessed, waxed, numbered—so politics could not swallow the truth later. Prisoners and evidence were marched back into Markarth's stone throat and delivered directly to Fasendil and Jarl Igmund. Corruption was no longer rumor. It was ink with a signature.

Markarth did not become safer. It became clearer.

And in the background, two hungrier truths remained:
Molag Bal's attention had become personal, pleased by "necessary" domination.
And someone in clean gloves had built a route designed to reach Solitude after the courier chain was compromised.

The war for Skyrim's holds is fought in sieges and duels—yes.
But also in tunnels, ledgers, and the quiet moment a man chooses coin over his own people.
